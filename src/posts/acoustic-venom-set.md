## About

A curated solo set concept: Nine Inch Nails meets acoustic coffee shop. Voice, guitar or piano, optional stomp box. The premise is industrial intimacy: dark alternative songs stripped to their skeleton and made heavier by the exposure. Late-night coffee shop, controlled catharsis. Not a gimmick. A test of which songs actually survive the strip-down and gain weight naked.

## The Central Rule

Choose songs not by vocal difficulty or cheap recognition, but by whether they earn their place with nothing to hide behind. A song survives acoustic venom if the melody was always the engine and the production was just weather. If the lyrics hit harder when you can understand every word. If the groove translates to palm mute, stomp box, or a left hand on piano.

The goal is emotional dissonance, not volume. Surprise, not novelty.

## Hard Curation Rules

Excluded outright: "Creep" (Radiohead), "Black Hole Sun," "Hurt" used as a centerpiece. "Hurt" is the "Hallelujah" of NIN covers: sincere the first time, then a shorthand for depth that delivers none. Also out: acoustic YouTube bait, songs that work only because the audience recognizes the sadness from the original recording, and novelty covers whose entire pitch is "look, I made metal acoustic."

Prioritized instead: songs with a strong melody buried under heavy production, lyrics that sound more brutal when spoken clearly, groove that lives in the body (palm mute, stomp, piano pulse), intimate verses with controlled-tension choruses, and emotional dissonance that does not need amplification.

## Performance Approach

Verses: close-mic, almost spoken. The melody is there but held back, like the song is telling you something it does not want to say out loud. Choruses: raw edge, but not screaming. A whisper to a controlled break, not a whisper to a scream. The tension lives in the contrast, not in the peak.

Guitar: heavy strings, drop D, palm mute as groove. Dark reverb, short delay. Piano when the song asks for it; the sustain pedal is a weapon.

Presence: seated, intense, dry humor between songs. One dry phrase of banter, maximum. Then the next song starts.

## Core Candidates

**"Personal Jesus" (Depeche Mode).** Blues, industrial, confident. The acoustic arrangement almost writes itself: the riff on guitar, the vocal half sneer and half sermon. An opener that establishes the room. The audience thinks they know this song; the arrangement proves they do not.

**"The Becoming" (NIN).** The mechanical pulse translates to stomp box plus palm mute. The lyrics about a body turning against itself become more disturbing at low volume, not less. The chorus breaks open just enough.

**"March of the Pigs" (NIN).** Extreme contrast. This is the risk. Acoustic does not mean slow. The time signature shifts and the dynamic jumps work if the right hand commits fully. If it lands, it redefines what the rest of the set can be. If it fails, it fails fast and the next song recovers.

**"Something I Can Never Have" (NIN).** Raw. Piano or guitar with nothing else. The original is already sparse; the acoustic version removes the reverb and the safety net. The silence between phrases does the work.

**"The Fragile" (NIN).** Delicate. The melody carries tenderness that the production softens. Stripped down, the fragility is genuine and uncomfortable. A closer or a near-closer.

## Why This Works

Industrial music at low volume is an act of trust with the audience. The songs were built to fill arenas with noise. Playing them in a room where you can hear breathing inverts the entire relationship between the performer, the song, and the listener. The weight does not come from decibels. It comes from the song proving it was always that heavy.
